\begin{abstract}

Sparse Oblique (SPO) splits that select linear combinations of multiple features are a powerful decision tree learner. Compared with axis-aligned forests that split single features, they typically achieve higher accuracy, limit overfitting, and maintain interpretability. 
Because sparse-oblique methods sample feature combinations at runtime, they preclude pre-sorting features or pre-computing histograms. This makes them more data and compute intensive.
We optimize the performance of sparse-oblique gradient boosted decision trees (GBDT) and random forests (RF).
For shallow trees of depth 6 (typical of GBDT), we achieve up to 2$\times$ speedup by vectorizing histogram construction,
For deeper trees of 16 levels up to full depth, typical of RF, we adaptively choose between histograms and exact splits at each node.

\section{Introduction}

% Para 1: Both RF and GBT are important even in the modern LLM world (compressed to five sentences).
Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT) and Random Forests (RF) remain critical components in machine learning ensembles. Though tabular foundation models outperform tuned tree ensembles when datasets are smaller than 50k rows and 2000 features~\cite{hollmann_accurate_2025,qu2026tabiclv2,grinsztajn2026tabpfn,tabarena,kong2026tabfm}, GBDTs perform best on high-dimensional datasets with millions to billions of rows~\cite{ye2026closer}. RF offers several benefits to GBDTs at the expense of inference latency. They are highly parallelizable, robust to hyperparameter selection~\cite{probst2019tunability} and give an unbiased error estimate and feature importances without holding out data, and adding trees does not overfit~\cite{breiman}. Due to their low computation cost, trees have a very strong cost-to-performance ratio, so are heavily used in resource usage-aware deployments~\cite{maier2026hapens}.

Sparse oblique (SPO) splitting further improves the expressivity and accuracy of GBDT and RF~\cite{tomita2020sparse}. While RF selects a random $\sqrt{d}$ subset of features at each node and evaluates which feature best separates the data, sparse oblique forests sample $\sqrt{d}$ random projections, consisting of a sparse linear combination of features. The stochasticity and greater expressivity of the projections makes sparse oblique trees robust to noise and on average leads to more balanced trees~\cite{tomita2020sparse}, and its interpretability makes it a strong candidate for biomedical benchmarks~\cite{might2}.

% SPO motivation without the AutoML figure: YDF KDD'23 Sec. 1 (AutoML share), Sec. 3.11 + Appendix C.1 (rank1 template = SPARSE_OBLIQUE for RF and GBT), Sec. 5 (benchmark hp slower to train).
Yggdrasil Decision Forests (YDF)~\cite{ydf} is Google's open-source implementation of decision forests, serving tens of millions of predictions per second at Google~\cite{ydf}, and offers a wide variety of GBDT and RF learners, including Sparse Oblique. YDF is integrated in Google's internal AutoML systems, where it is chosen in roughly a quarter of real-world use cases~\cite{ydf}. Sparse oblique splits are in YDF's top-ranked benchmark configuration for both RF and GBDT, and ranks above every untuned XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} and scikit-learn learner~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}. We include a similar comparison, adding CatBoost~\cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost} (see Table~\ref{tab:auc-vs-libraries-per-dataset-auc}). The YDF authors report that this configuration is significantly slower to train than their defaults (~\cite{ydf}, Table~\ref{tab:train-time-vs-libraries}).

Given the importance of SPO methods in YDF, we study how to reduce or eliminate training time overhead. Unlike a regular, feature axis-aligned (AA) decision tree, where the feature set remains constant throughout training, oblique random projections must be performed at runtime. At each node, projection features are randomly sampled, their linear combination is calculated, and the resulting new ``feature'' is scanned for the best split. There are combinatorially many such splits in the number of features. The evaluation of this larger feature space gives SPO its accuracy benefit, but precludes many optimization strategies such as pre-sorting features for exact splits~\cite{ydf,wright2017ranger}, histogram subtraction~\cite{chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm}, or oblivious trees~\cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost}.

We contributed the fixes to YDF (Floyd's projections sampling, caching projection evaluator, a Fisher-Yates shuffle, among others) as a series of pull requests (see Appendix~\ref{app:pull-requests} for full list). Independently, Google replaced \texttt{std::sort} with Highway VQSort~\cite{wassenberg_vectorized_2022}, a highly optimized sorting library that heavily leverages SIMD instructions, which we adopted. Together these set a new baseline for this paper that is 2-5$\times$ faster than the open-source version with which we started. In this baseline, the majority of runtime is spent sorting nodes with {\em exact} splits: Highway sort is faster than every prior method, including all histogram variants and \texttt{std::sort} (Table~\ref{tab:stdsort-vs-highway}). All speedups in this paper are measured against this stronger baseline. Training time not only depends on tree depth, but is skewed to shallower or deeper levels depending on the splitting method:  very large nodes near the root disproportionately affect the $O(n\log n)$ sorting cost, while large numbers of small nodes punish the $O(k)$ startup cost in histogram's $O(n\log k + k)$ complexity. GBTs are trained to depth 6, often to leverage a fast inference engine, such as QuickScorer~\cite{lucchese_quickscorer_2015}, which has a limit on maximum number of nodes. Training to depth 16-24 is common for Random Forests, and training to purity (full depth) is useful for posterior estimates~\cite{might1,might2}. The two families thus stress opposite ends of the depth range, and we contribute one optimization for each. Based on this observation, we contribute:

\emph{\textbf{(1) Vectorization of histogram construction}}. Histogram construction is asymptotically more efficient than sorting and should be faster when there are a sufficient number of samples. Yet with the powerful Highway sort replacing \texttt{std::sort}, the baseline histogram implementation using \texttt{std::upper\_bound()} is much slower than vectorized Highway sort (Figure~\ref{fig:dynamic-motivation-nodetrain}). We propose a hierarchical vectorized histogram filling implementation that leverages AVX2 and AVX-512, and delivers up to $2\times$ speedup over the SPO-RF Exact and $1.6\times$ over SPO-GBT, and beats scalar Random Hist. at all tree levels, while being mathematically identical.

\emph{\textbf{(2) Runtime-adaptive histograms.}} In deeper trees, the number of active nodes grows near-exponentially, and peaks at around halfway to purity. Though these nodes have few samples in them, histogram approaches must still pay the $O(k)$ startup cost of bin generation and scanning that Exact avoids (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic-motivation-nodetrain}). We propose a Dynamic switching method that adaptively chooses between vectorized histograms and exact splits depending on the number of samples at each node, with no detectable effect on accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:accuracy-rf}). This achieves up to 1.3$\times$ speedup when training deeper trees, bringing SPO performance on par with Axis-Aligned in YDF (Table~\ref{tab:train-time-vs-libraries}).

Neither optimization is tied to a depth or a model family. Vectorized filling accelerates every histogram split, and the dynamic switch acts on node size rather than depth, so both apply to GBDT and RF alike, and to axis-aligned splits whenever features are not pre-sorted (Figure~\ref{fig:dynamic-motivation-nodetrain}(c)). At depth 6 over millions of rows no node is small enough to switch, so dynamic selection reduces to vectorized histograms, and at purity, all nodes switch. We therefore report each family in the setting it is used in practice, GBDT at depth 6 and RF from depth 16 to purity. We provide extensive results on a variety of both natural and synthetic datasets (Table ~\ref{tab:all-datasets}), we sweep the full depth range in Table~\ref{tab:train-time-by-depth}. In addition, we provide a heatmap of predicted speedups by dataset size based on our evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:speedup-heatmap}). An open-source implementation is available at (anonymized).

Figure~\ref{fig:dynamic-motivation} reveals histograms for approximate splitting and sorting for exact splitting have different costs at different levels in the tree. Conventional wisdom is that histograms are faster, require less memory, and perform favorable sequential memory access~\cite{ydf,prokhorenkova2018catboost}.
While this is true for nodes higher in the tree with many active samples--the $O(n\log n)$ complexity of sorting dominates the $O(k+n\log k)$ cost of filling a $k$-bin histogram--down the tree, when the number of active samples in each node becomes small and the number of nodes grows large, the fixed cost of allocating and initializing histograms dominates. This leads to the core idea of using histograms for high cardinality nodes and sorting for low cardinality nodes. The resulting dynamic histograms track the best performance of both splitting methods. Although adaptively switching between the two techniques changes the algorithm, we reveal only minor variances in accuracy from either splitting technique.

The MIGHT algorithm~\cite{might1,might2} gives provable guarantees on uncertainty and confidence while controlling for specific error types. This is particularly valuable in biomedical applications, such as cancer screening, in which false positives are minimized in favor of false negatives. These methods use sparse oblique random forests~\cite{tomita2020sparse} as a base non-parametric learning algorithm and add canonical cross validation and parametric calibration. The MIGHT algorithm results in coefficients of variation orders of magnitude less than transformers, support vector machines, or random forests at the same or better sensitivity~\cite{might1}.

To realize these guarantees, MIGHT defines a workload that is more data and compute intensive than gradient-boosted trees (GBT) or random forests (RF). MIGHT divides the bootstrap sample into three sets for training, calibration, and validation. MIGHT enhances the standard random-forest algorithm to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item\label{it:sparsity} evaluate sparse, random combinations of variables at each tree node to find splitting features.
    \item\label{it:purity} train each tree to {\em purity} so that each leaf node in the tree contains training samples data from a single class.
    \item estimate classification probabilities at each node by fitting posteriors on the calibration set.
    \item score samples from the validation set using kernel prediction~\cite{kernel}.
\end{enumerate}
%These changes have profound implications on the computation and the resulting trees.

\noindent The original implementation of MIGHT was built on the scikit-learn toolkit~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit,treeple} and took over 8 hours to train on the Wise-1 dataset with 2523 features and 352 data points. Our goal is to train a classifier for an upcoming dataset with >440,000 gene expression features, necessitating this work.

Mainstream tree ensembles search dataset features for the one that best splits the classes. These splits, done exclusively in the range of the chosen feature, are termed axis-aligned. Random forest methods use exact splits by sorting \cite{wright2017ranger} or by both sorting and histogramming \cite{ydf,pedregosa2011scikit}. Most gradient-boosted tree methods rely on histograms~\cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost,ponomareva2017tf,ke2017lightgbm,wenthundergbm19,rapids},
although some provide both~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit,chen2016xgboost,wenthundergbm19}. To our knowledge, no existing method dynamically chooses the optimal method per node during training.

Oblique decision trees replace axis-aligned thresholds with linear combinations of sparse subsets of features, increasing expressivity and leading to shallower trees. Bivariate Decision Trees are a special case of SPO~\cite{kairgeldin_bivariate_2024}.

With the advent of big data it became important to have tree ensembles that scale well in terms of number of features or dimension. Towards this end, Ranger~\cite{wright2017ranger} implemented parallel, multi-threaded training and combined pre-sorted data for high-cardinality nodes with in-node sorting for small nodes. YDF~\cite{ydf} extends this method to data that exceeds memory capacity.

    Presorting data~\cite{ydf,pedregosa2011scikit,chen2016xgboost,wenthundergbm19} ensures that samples of a feature after splits remain sorted. This is critical when dealing with large datasets. This cannot be applied to sparse oblique forests, as projections are sampled per-node.

Inference performance optimization of RF and GBT includes: trees being compiled into C++~\cite{cho2018treelite,rapids} to improve memory layout and reduce branching and into tensor operations~\cite{nakandala2020tensor}. Cache-efficient memory layouts have been applied to nodes in multiple trees to reduce random I/O~\cite{browne2022forestpacking,madhyastha2021blockset}.

\subsection{Instrumenting Sparse-Oblique}

We identified bottlenecks and optimization opportunities in sparse oblique YDF (SO-YDF) by implementing full timing instrumentation. We did this for histograms and exact splits and measured at all nodes in the tree.
The computation at each node involves trying many linear combinations of features to identify the best splitting features. The first step is to build a projection matrix:
a sparse list that samples uniformly with replacement. The default samples $3 \sqrt{d}$ non-zero offsets from a  matrix of $d$ features by $1.5 \sqrt{d}$ rows and randomly assigns weights~\cite{tomita2020sparse}. Then for each row in the projection matrix, we evaluate the quality of the combination (Figure \ref{fig:computenode}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build the feature by combining the active samples of the data column for the non-zero offsets and apply weights. This is a sparse vector-sum of, on average, $1.5 \sqrt{d}$ $n$-row columns that returns a dense vector.
\item Build a histogram of the projected feature that assigns samples to bins and counts the class instances in each bin.
\item Evaluate a splitting criteria (entropy in YDF) at each split and return the boundary that best separates the data.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{\textit{this applies to everything: RF, SPORF and GBT}}

YDF's implementation of histograms uses C++ {\tt std:upper\_bound()} to conduct binary search among the bin boundaries to identify a bin in to which to update a count for a class. In addition to taking steps logarithmic in the number of bins, the branches in binary search are taken with equal probability, have low predictability, and result in pipeline stalls. An alternative is to scan the bins, which has higher predictability, but performs more work. Scanning is better for small histograms up to 16 or 32 bins.

Tree ensembles choose a modest number of bins that balance computation and space versus overfitting the data. Inaccuracies from fewer bins can be resolved deeper in the tree or in other trees. Default values are as low as 20 (H2O) or 32 (Spark MLLib), but are most often 64 or 256 (YDF, CatBoost, XGBoost).

{\bf Contribution 1 (Vectorization of histogram construction):} We observed that binary search can be replaced by a hierarchy of vector operations that compare multiple bin boundaries simultaneously. A 16-wide AVX-512 vector can route values into 256 histogram bins in two operations by selecting a coarse-grained region of 16 bins in the first compare and then refining to a single bin in the second. This replaces an inner loop of 42 instructions with branch misses into a 7 instruction loop with no branches, resulting in a 2$\times$ improvement in histogram construction and 1.5 $\times$ end-to-end speedup for GBDT.

The concept is to break the boundaries into 16 groups of 16. We do a coarse-grained compare to select the group and a fine-grained compare to locate the bin. The data structure resembles a two-level deterministic skip list. Once per histogram, we construct a coarse-grained vector that describes the boundary of every $16^{\rm th}$ bin. We also provide a 64 bin, 8-bit wide AVX2 implementation. Figure~\ref{fig:vectorization-illustration} compares the AVX-implementation with binary search over 64 bins. The binary search uses 7 instructions with one branch per node for a minimum of 42 serial instructions and not branch prediction-friendly. Miss rate of the binary search depends on the skewness of the child nodes: skewed subtrees have higher prediction, while even splits approach ~50\% miss rate. AVX2 broadcasts the search value to a vector. Does a parallel compare and resolves the coarse grained bin using a mask and count intrinsic. It repeats this at a finer grain for a total of 16 instructions.

The exact cardinality varies depending on the system architecture. We determine the crossover value using a simple microbenchmark that runs one time at the start of training. It applies to all nodes of all trees.

Our study revealed performance concerns when training SO-YDF trees to purity. When the number of active samples is small, histogram construction dominates runtime. There are fixed costs to set up a histogram and these are incurred at each node. Referring back to Figure \ref{fig:dynamic-motivation}, histograms operate relatively much faster at the top levels of the tree when the number of samples are large. These benefits endure through the first 12-13 levels of the tree on a 1M-row balanced dataset. Below this level, there are many active nodes, each of which requires a histogram. Here, sorting few samples can be much faster than building a histogram. %Implementations of {\sf std::sort()} have many optimizations to sort few elements, including unguarded insertions.

The  breakeven point depends on the number of active samples and the  hardware. Tree depth is just our proxy for visualizing the total runtime. Figure \ref{fig:threshold-sensitivity} show the relative cost of exact splits versus histograms on server CPU, showing a breakeven point of just over 1200 points.

The break-even point is determined by a microbenchmark that runs at the start of training. This takes less than 100ms to perform a binary search over reasonable parameters.

% Our results will show that perforamnce is robust over a range on this parameter, i.e. not hard to tune.  

During tree-construction, we dynamically choose between a histogram and sorting on a node-by-node basis. Two nodes at the same tree depth may use different techniques (Figure \ref{fig:levels}). From a performance standpoint, this solves the problem with creating deep trees.  We avoid the overhead of sorting at high cardinality nodes and of histograms for low cardinality nodes. There is only a small performance penalty for training to purity.  Nodes beyond depth 20 use less 5\% of the runtime (Figure \ref{fig:threshold-sensitivity}).

We experiment to find the inflection point (Figure \ref{fig:threshold-sensitivity}), revealing it to be $\sim 350$ for Random Histograms and $\sim 200$ for Vectorized Random histograms, due to the latter being faster.

Dynamic histograms produce different trees and potentially different learning outcomes. The resulting forests are most similar to those constructed by histograms. The high levels of the trees are the same. They differ only in the low cardinality nodes when exact splitting chooses a boundary that separates elements that are in the same bucket. The accuracy of all methods; histograms, exact, and dynamic, are statistically indistinguishable.

\subsection{YDF Pull Request Contributions}
\label{app:pull-requests}

\textit{Due to anonymity concerns with exact Pull-request numbers and titles, we only provide a summary below.}

This project has resulted in numerous improvements to the YDF library, contributed as pull requests:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Candidate-attribute sampling.} The eager per-node shuffle of all attributes is replaced by a lazy prefix Fisher--Yates shuffle that only draws the few positions actually tested, and is up to 75\% faster on high-dimensional data.
    \item \textbf{Projection-evaluator caching.} We replaced an $O(F)$ object re-initialization with a cached and reused object per thread. Up to 3$\times$ faster on high-dimensional data.
    \item \textbf{Random-histogram split search.} Invariant code hoisted out of the candidate-threshold scan - up to 30\% for sparse oblique speedup.
    \item \textbf{Vectorized bin generation.} \texttt{std::sort} is replaced by Highway VQSort in generating histogram bins; up to 15\%.
    \item \textbf{Histogram split search for oblique forests.} Adds the histogram (approximate) split finder to the sparse-oblique path, which previously supported only exact splits.
    \item \textbf{Equal-width binning.} Samples are assigned to equal-width bins with $O(1)$ arithmetic instead of a binary search over the bin boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Projection sampling.} The per-feature uniform draws that build each sparse projection are replaced by one Binomial draw for the number of non-zeros plus Floyd's sampler for their positions, reducing the cost from linear in the number of features to linear in the number of non-zeros (proof below).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Floyd's projection sampling.}
Originally, random sampling to build the projection matrix was a computational bottleneck for SO-YDF. This component comprised 80\% of the runtime, with the math-heavy parts of the code taking <20\%. The previous design assumed small $d$ and was inefficient for wide data.

Existing code calls Unif(0,1) over the projection matrix to create a mask, then populates this mask with weights. However, this requires $\Theta(np)$ calls to {\tt Unif($\cdot$)}, or $p$ calls per each projection, across $n$ projections. We show this can be substituted with a single call to Binomial($np, n\frac{k}{p}$), cutting runtime by 33\% for our settings, proportional to $\Theta(np)$.

Using a variant of an algorithm from Floyd~\cite{10.1145/30401.315746}, we form the projection matrix as follows:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=c++]
projection_density = k/p;
for projection in projections:  // O(n)
    for feature in features:    // O(p)
        if (Unif01(.) < projection_density) {...}
\end{lstlisting}
