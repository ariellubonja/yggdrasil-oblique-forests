Paper Summary:
The authors propose an improved method to find the splits when learning oblique trees. This method switches dynamically between sorting and historgrams to do so. This switching is based on the observation that existing methods use histograms when dealing with large data while the more sparse nodes would benefit from a sorting technique. A highly optimized implementation is presented.

Paper Strengths:
This work provides a faster method to learn oblique trees, a method that is known to be computationally expensive but with better expressivity.

The method is clear from the description and the improvements are experimentally validated.

Paper Weaknesses:
The improvement is for one particular technique. Given that this technique is also not that common, a more extensive evaluation is warranted. It should also be motivated why this speedup is providing an advantage compare to other techniques (i.e. if the performance of the base technique is not sufficient, why speed it up). For example, axis based techniques such as XGBoost. Is the YDF technique with a 2x speedup better than XGBT or other techniques (speed and accuracy wise)? The authors have space left, thus this cannot be a space issue (thus even if the YDF paper shows performance, it would have been good to reconfirm these results given that there is space to show results).

The experiments do not really prove the point that there is an issue with large datasets and runtime. For example, table 2 has datasets that require only a bit over 5 minutes of training time. There is also something wrong in Table 3. there is no GPU speedup for Trunk-10M but a slowdown. The accuracy comparison is also performed on 'smaller OpenML datasets'. Why if the purpose of the method is to scale up?

In the appendix, it is not clear how this links to Floyd's algorithm. I understand what the idea is but this does not follow from the pseudocode. Floyd's algorithm has as many calls for a random number as there are elements required. Here the call is still within the double loop, so no calls are saved. Something seems missing.

Overall, the paper seems incomplete and not yet mature. There is a neat idea and a good execution of the code, but inconsistent experimentation for an implementation focused contribution.

Paper Summary:
This paper speeds up sparse oblique forest training for the MIGHT algorithm in YDF. It combines: (1) dynamic switching between exact sorting and histograms by node size, (2) AVX-2 vectorized histogram construction, and (3) hybrid CPU-GPU execution that offloads large nodes to the GPU. The paper reports 1.7-2.5x end-to-end speedup over SO-YDF exact on large datasets, with no meaningful accuracy loss.

Paper Strengths:
S1. It targets a real bottleneck in MIGHT, improving practicality for very high-dimensional data. S2. The three optimizations are distinct, motivated, and ablated via microbenchmarks. S3. Table 4 suggests dynamic histograms preserve accuracy relative to exact splits on the tested datasets.

Paper Weaknesses:
W1. The novelty is low and the ideas are mostly established systems techniques. The core idea is straightforward: histograms are better for large nodes, sorting is better for small nodes, so the system switches between them. SIMD vectorization and CPU/GPU dispatch are also standard optimization techniques. The contribution is therefore mainly an engineering combination for SO-YDF, not a new algorithmic or data mining insight. W2. The adaptive switching rule is simplistic and underanalyzed. The "dynamic" part is essentially a one-time crossover threshold chosen by a startup microbenchmark, then applied using node cardinality during training. The paper does not analyze whether this rule remains reliable when the effective scenario changes quickly across nodes or depends on other factors such as projection count, feature dimensionality, sparsity, cache behavior, or contention. It is therefore unclear how robust the switching policy is beyond the tested setup. W3. The experimental coverage of scenarios is not convincing. The paper argues that high-row, low-dimensional datasets are a reasonable proxy for the large-wide biomedical setting that motivates the work, but this is only an assumption. Most performance evidence comes from HIGGS, SUSY, Epsilon, and synthetic Trunk rather than truly ultra-wide real datasets. The paper also does not show that the chosen datasets cover the main regimes where sorting should win, histograms should win, and the crossover should matter. W4. The accuracy evaluation is weak for the target use case, and Table 4 is itself somewhat suspicious. Accuracy is evaluated mainly on small OpenML datasets, not on the large settings used for the performance claims and not on the biomedical-scale workloads motivating the paper. In Table 4, several methods appear to have exactly the same accuracy on multiple datasets, which is unusual and needs explanation: it is unclear whether this is due to rounding, averaging over runs, deterministic training behavior, or some reporting artifact. The paper states that differences are statistically indistinguishable, but it does not report significance testing details in a convincing way for the target setting. W5. The GPU contribution is modest on real datasets. GPU acceleration is only 11.1% on HIGGS, 6.95% on SUSY, 0.75% on Epsilon, and 2.02% on Trunk-100k. The much larger gain is on synthetic Trunk-10M. Since the GPU only helps the largest nodes and occupancy stays below 70%, the practical value of the GPU contribution is limited in the real benchmarks shown. W6. The paper lacks competitive baseline context. The paper compares mainly against SO-YDF exact and RF exact. Without comparison to optimized tree learners such as LightGBM, XGBoost, or CatBoost, it is hard to judge whether a 1.7-2.5x speedup over SO-YDF is practically meaningful. W7. Table 4 appears after the references in the submitted PDF, which is not proper paper formatting and makes the presentation look careless.

Paper Summary:
The paper presents an optimization training algorithm implementation for sparse oblique forests in the Yggdrasil Random Forest framework via adaptive histogram computation and a vectorization. To do so, the authors first note that there is a break-even point in the runtime when using histograms for tree induction and feature sorting in which feature sorting becomes more efficient when less data is available (typically happening further down the tree). Naturally, they propose to combine both aspects and start with histogram computations and then dynamically switch to feature sorting at some point. Second, they propose to vectorize histogram filling by a two step approach: first, items are sorted in more coarse-grained bins (i.e. a group of bins), and then, in a second step, a more fine-grained sorting is used to find the actual bin. With clever use of SIMD instructions, this approach becomes faster than a direct binary search. Finally, the authors also discuss a hybrid CPU-GPU implementation of their approach and perform extensive benchmarking in the experimental section. Expectantly, the proposed approach is quicker than current implementations.

Paper Strengths:
Well-written paper that is complex, but easy to follow
While the dynamic histogram idea is somewhat straightforward, the benchmarking and evaluation to find the break-even point is quite complex to pull off in practice
This approach is a valuable addition to the Yggdrasil Random Forest framework, and the proposed optimizations can also be interesting to other (oblique) tree-based methods
Paper Weaknesses:
While the paper is generally well-written, it refers to the MIGHT algorithm in the intro and background section, with a strange way of explaining it. I am still unsure what the overlap between MIGHT and oblique RFs are

Paper Weaknesses:
The contribution of this paper is small. The updates to Sparse Oblique Forests are only engineering changes. For example, the method uses SIMD vectorized histograms and shares tasks between GPUs and CPUs. These changes make the model faster, but they do not add new theory contribution.

The paper lacks a clear problem definition and enough background information to explain the new method. The text must be complete on its own, so readers do not need to read other papers to understand the work. Following the KDD template, you should have two more pages for further explanation.

The crossover point comes from a microbenchmark that runs once per machine, but data shape and node size vary during training, so this fixed threshold may be suboptimal. A sensitivity study or an online cost model would improve confidence.
